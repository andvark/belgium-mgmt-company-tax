## 1. What is compared

- **Same economic activity.** *Profit level* = expected annual revenue billed to the client (for the employee: total employer cost). All arrangements face the same revenue path each year (paired shocks: replicate-mean exactly 1; employee gets the wage residual, business forms also the business shock), the same household and the same starting wealth. Horizon 2026-2040 (15 years), constant 2026 euros, **no indexation of thresholds**.
- **Household** = household spending need (placeholder: 24,000 first adult, +12,000 partner, +6,000 per child), minus an employed partner's fixed net income (placeholder 30,000). Under the rules taxation is individual, so household type changes the owner's outcome **only through this liquidity constraint**; family tax rules (child allowances, marital quotient) are not modelled.
- **Cohort**: *mature* = the company is already in its fifth taxable period in 2026 (the EUR 51,000 pay condition applies from year 1); *new* = founded 2026 (periods 1-4 exempt from the pay condition; VVPRbis at 18% only for profit of the third accounting year after the contribution year, i.e. from 2029).
- **BV strategies** (19): pay rule (`thr` = the cheapest pay that keeps the 20% rate: EUR 51,000, or - below EUR 102,000 of profit before pay - half of that profit, because below EUR 51,000 pay must cover the taxable income left after it; `min` = smallest pay that finances the household need, i.e. the 'minimum salary' strategy; `shell` = all profit paid out) x what happens to post-tax profit (annual dividend; retained in the free reserve; allocated to the liquidation reserve) x exit route (VVPRbis 18% dividend, ordinary 30% liquidation, liquidation reserve paid out on liquidation (9.09% in total), liquidation-reserve dividend after the lock (18.00% in total)), with interim lumps after 3 or 5 years. `BV_BEST` is the strategy with the highest mean terminal wealth in the cell (anti-abuse variants excluded).
- **Metric** = terminal wealth in 2040 after liquidating the company and paying every tax (private wealth + company, same spending in all arrangements), paired against the sole trader / employee in the same replicate. Positive = better than the comparator. Real return on retained funds = **0%** in the baseline (so the timing of extraction has no value by construction; see sensitivities).

Key to names: `thr`/`min` pay rule; `dist_ord`/`dist_vvpr` dividend every year at 30% / 18%; `ret_vvpr_*` retain and take a VVPRbis dividend at the end / after 3 / after 5 years; `ret_ord_liq` retain and liquidate at 30%; `lr_liq` liquidation reserve then liquidation (9.09% in total); `lr_div_k5` liquidation reserve with a dividend after 5 years of the vintages past the lock (18.00% on those); `*_antiabuse` the liquidation payout is taxed as an ordinary dividend (30%, a scenario: the consequence is not fixed and the timing is not modelled).

**Findings that follow mechanically from the rules**

1. With a 0% return, *retaining then paying a VVPRbis dividend after 3, 5 or 15 years gives exactly the same result as paying the 18% dividend every year* (`ret_vvpr_*` = `dist_vvpr`): the tax advantage comes from the **rate gap** between the 18% (or 9.1%) exit and the 45-50% marginal personal tax plus contributions on pay, not from deferral. Deferral only matters through returns on retained funds (section 6).
2. Without the reduced VVPRbis rate (`dist_ord` / `ret_ord_liq`, 30%) the advantage over the sole trader is roughly halved at EUR 100,000 revenue; it stays positive.
3. The liquidation-reserve-then-liquidation route (9.09% in total) is the cheapest exit at every profit level. If the 2026 anti-abuse rule applies (owner becomes director of a similar company within 3 years - plausible for a consultant who continues working), the route loses most of its value (payout assumed taxed at 30% at liquidation; the real rule taxes it as movable income in the later year, which the model does not time).
4. For the single owner the best strategy is `thr_lr_liq` at every revenue level: pay the cheapest salary that keeps the 20% rate (EUR 51,000, or half of profit before pay below EUR 102,000) and put the rest in the liquidation reserve. Paying only what the household needs (`min`) is no longer better, because it gives up the 20% rate; in comparison B it wins only for the two-earner household, where the partner covers most of the household's spending.

## 7. What this does and does not show

- It shows the **size and structure** of the BV advantage that the *supplied* rules imply, by profit level, strategy and exit route. For a single owner it is 15,203 a year at EUR 100,000 of revenue (15.2%) rising to 50,135 at EUR 250,000 (20.1%) against a sole trader. At EUR 100,000, 44.8% of the total tax-and-contribution saving is lower social contributions (section 4); the rest comes from the gap between the 10-18% exit rates and the 45-50% marginal rate on pay.
- It does **not** show how many owners behave this way, nor that real owners attain these results (perfect foresight, perfect timing, VVPRbis eligibility assumed, no pension saving for the comparators, no third-pillar saving or individual pension commitment, no indexation).
- **Not modelled, all of which could change the level:** whether a given owner meets the VVPRbis conditions (assumed met; the three-year wait is modelled); the 20% benefits-in-kind rule on director remuneration; start-up exemption from the company contribution; tax-advantaged private pension saving for sole traders and employees; family tax rules; municipal differences (the 7.2% average could not be reproduced); social-fund fees; VAT; losses carried forward; the work bonus and employer reductions for the employee; the timing of the anti-abuse rule.
- **Rules.** Most rules were reported confirmed in the owner's checklist review (15 November 2026); two earlier readings were corrected by it (the liquidation-reserve total is 18.00%, and the lower-pay test uses profit after pay), and the lock, VVPRbis wait and employee lump sum were added. The remaining items to look at are in `hand_check_list.md`.
